% 6. Conclusion
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Back to the bottle in the roof. It is still, to me, one of the most
satisfying pieces of design I know: a problem understood so precisely that
the answer costs almost nothing. But after working through the evidence I see
it differently from when I started. The bottle light works because Moser had
a tin roof, a plastic bottle, bleach, daylight and the time to think about
them. It does nothing at night, nothing for a house with a concrete ceiling,
nothing for a family whose problem is not a dark room but an empty one. It is
a good answer to a narrow question, and its spread depended on a foundation
deciding to spread it. All of that is consistent with the thesis, but it is a
smaller thesis than the one I began with.

So let me state it again, more carefully than I did in the introduction.

\begin{keyidea}
When a system that the standard solution depends on is missing, but people
still have basic tools, some slack and a path to a wider market, the problem
sometimes gets reframed around what people actually need. The resulting
solutions tend to be cheaper, narrower and more widely usable than the
original, and occasionally they travel back. Far more often, the missing
system simply means going without.
\end{keyidea}

That is less exciting than ``necessity is the mother of invention'', and I
think that is a point in its favour. It explains why the same scarcity
produces M-Pesa in one place and nothing in another. It explains why the
psychology finds constraints helpful in the laboratory and harmful in real
poverty. And it leaves no room for the idea that hardship is good for people,
because the conditions it names, tools, slack, room and connection, are all
forms of investment.

\subsection{Open questions}

Three questions seem to me both important and answerable.

\emph{Where is the peak of the inverted U?} The constraints literature
proposes that moderate constraint helps and severe constraint hurts, but no one
has located the turning point for households or firms facing real
deprivation. Studies that follow the same communities as their circumstances
change, like the before-and-after-harvest design in \textcite{mani2013}, could
start to answer it.

\emph{How large is the graveyard?} I have no systematic data on frugal
innovations that failed. A census of attempts, including the bottle lights
that leaked, the kits that broke and the mobile-money launches that never
took off, would tell us how unusual the successes are.

\emph{How much does reverse flow really happen?} The few well-documented
cases, such as GE's portable medical devices, come from firms with an
interest in telling the story. A careful count of products that moved from
low-income to high-income markets, and of how they fared, would test the third
part of the mechanism directly.

\subsection{What would change my mind}

An argument made in good faith should say what would weaken it. Here is what
would weaken this one.

If careful studies showed that the spread of off-grid solar and mobile money
is explained almost entirely by things unrelated to the missing default,
such as regulation, falling component prices and phone ownership, so that
places \emph{with} good grids and banks adopted these tools just as fast, then
the removal-of-the-default step would be doing no work, and the thesis would
reduce to ``cheap technology spreads''. I would accept that.

If a census of attempts showed that successes are no more common under
scarcity than under abundance, once you account for how many attempts are made,
then scarcity would be telling us nothing about innovation and only about
which stories get told.

And if the conditions I have proposed in \cref{sec:synthesis} turned out not
to distinguish the successes from the failures, the synthesis would be
wrong even if the individual cases are right.

Until then, I think the most honest summary is this. Scarcity is not a gift,
and nobody should be asked to be grateful for it. But where it exists, it
sometimes asks a better question than abundance does. The light bulb answered
``how do we light a room?'' with a grid. The bottle answered it with the sun.
Both answers are useful, and we will need both.
