\noindent{\large\color{indigo}\itshape
Scarcity does not produce \emph{more} innovation. Sometimes it produces a
\emph{different kind}.\par}
\vspace{12pt}

\noindent
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.485\linewidth}
\vspace{0pt}
\begin{glancebox}{indigo}
\textbf{\sffamily Removal of the default.} When the system a standard solution
depends on is missing (a grid, a bank branch, a hospital), the standard
solution is off the table.
\end{glancebox}
\begin{glancebox}{indigo}
\textbf{\sffamily Reframing.} The question shifts from ``how do we copy it?'' to
``what do we actually need?'', and narrower needs have cheaper answers: a solar
kit instead of a grid, a phone instead of a branch.
\end{glancebox}
\begin{glancebox}{amber}
\textbf{\sffamily Reverse flow.} A few of these answers travel back to rich
markets, such as portable medical devices first built for rural India and
China \parencite{immelt2009}.
\end{glancebox}
\begin{glancebox}{indigo}
\textbf{\sffamily When it works.} Basic tools are available, need is dense and
shared, people have room to experiment, and there is a path to scale.
\end{glancebox}
\end{minipage}\hfill

\begin{glancebox}{clay}
\textbf{\sffamily\color{clay}Strongest counterargument.} Most of the time,
scarcity just means going without. In sub-Saharan Africa about
\sspWithoutElec{} people still have no electricity, more than in
\sspElecStartYear{}, and the cheap solar kits that look like scarcity-born
inventions rest on semiconductor and battery industries built by abundance.
