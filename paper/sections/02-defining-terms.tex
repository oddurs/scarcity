% 2. Defining Terms
\section{Defining Terms}
\label{sec:terms}

Words like ``scarcity'' and ``innovation'' carry a lot of weight in arguments
like this one, and they are easy to stretch until they mean whatever the
argument needs. So before making the case, I want to pin a few of them down.

\subsection{Two kinds of scarcity}

By \emph{absolute scarcity} I mean that the resource is simply not there. There
is no grid line within reach of the village, no bank branch in the district, no
eye surgeon in the province. By \emph{relative scarcity} I mean that the
resource exists but is out of reach: the grid passes overhead but the
connection fee is a year's income, or the hospital is in town but the
operation costs more than the household earns. The distinction matters because
the two produce different pressures. Absolute scarcity removes the standard
solution entirely. Relative scarcity leaves it visible but unaffordable, which
tends to produce cheaper copies rather than new designs. Most of the cases in
this paper involve a mixture, and I will try to say which is doing the work.

\subsection{Invention, adaptation and diffusion}

It also helps to separate three things that the word ``innovation'' blurs
together. \emph{Invention} is the creation of something genuinely new.
\emph{Adaptation} is the reworking of something that already exists so that it
fits a different setting. \emph{Diffusion} is the spread of a solution to the
people who use it. Very little of what follows is invention in the strict
sense. Skylights are ancient; mobile phones, text messages and solar cells were
all invented in rich countries. What scarcity seems to drive is mostly
adaptation and diffusion: new combinations of existing parts, and new ways of
getting them to people. That is not a lesser achievement. For the people
involved, a working combination that reaches them matters more than a novel
one that does not.

\subsection{Neighbouring ideas}

This argument sits among several existing ones, and borrows from all of them.

\textbf{Frugal innovation and jugaad.} The management literature has a rich
vocabulary for doing more with less. \textcite{radjou2012} popularised the
Hindi word \emph{jugaad}, an improvised fix, as a model for flexible,
low-cost problem-solving that Western firms should learn from. The idea has
critics. \textcite{birtchnell2011} argues that jugaad is better understood as a
symptom of systemic risk, of infrastructure and institutions that fail often
enough that improvisation becomes a way of life, and that celebrating it can
paper over the failure. I take both views seriously, and the second one will
come back in \cref{sec:against}.

\textbf{Reverse innovation.} \textcite{govindarajan2012} describe products
developed first for emerging markets that later find buyers in rich ones. Their
best-known example is from General Electric, which developed a handheld
electrocardiogram for rural India priced at about \geEcgPrice{} and a
portable ultrasound for China selling for as little as \geUltrasoundPrice{},
both of which were later sold in the United States
\parencite{immelt2009}. Reverse innovation is the third part of my mechanism,
but I am interested in it less as a corporate strategy than as evidence that
some constraint-born designs are simply better for a wider range of people.

\textbf{Leapfrogging.} A country leapfrogs when it skips a stage of technology
that richer countries passed through. The cleanest case is the telephone.
Across sub-Saharan Africa, fixed telephone lines never reached more than
\sspFixedPeak{} per 100 people, a peak hit in \sspFixedPeakYear{}; in the
United States they peaked at about \usaFixedPeak{} per 100. Mobile
subscriptions in the region now stand at about \sspMobileSubs{} per 100
\parencite{worldbank_wdi}, which overstates the number of people with a phone,
since many hold several SIM cards, but not by enough to change the story. Most
of the region went straight from no phone to a mobile phone
\parencite{aker2010}. Leapfrogging matters here because it is how the absence
of an old system becomes an opening for a new one.

\textbf{Constraint-based creativity.} Finally, there is a psychological
literature on how limits affect creative work. Its central finding is that
some constraints help: they block the familiar route and force people to
search more widely \parencite{stokes2006,moreau2005,mehta2016}. I come back to
this in \cref{sec:psychology}, because it also contains the clearest warning
that too much constraint does the opposite \parencite{acar2019}.

\subsection{The mechanism}

With those terms in hand, the argument can be stated as a mechanism with three
moving parts (\cref{fig:mechanism}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\input{figures/fig-mechanism}
\caption{\takeaway{The mechanism has three parts, and the most common outcome
is not on the main path.} When the default solution is missing, people usually
go without. Occasionally the problem is reframed and a different, cheaper
solution appears, and a few of those travel back to resource-rich places.
\figsource{Author's framework.}}
\label{fig:mechanism}
\end{figure}

\emph{First, removal of the default.} The inherited solution, whether a grid,
a bank branch or a hospital, is unavailable. This is what scarcity means in
practice: not a general shortage of everything, but the absence of the
particular system that the standard answer assumes.

\emph{Second, reframing.} With the default gone, the question shifts from
``how do we replicate X?'' to ``what do we actually need?'' A household does
not need a grid; it needs light, a charged phone and perhaps a radio. A trader
does not need a bank; she needs to send money safely and store it. A person
with cataracts does not need a teaching hospital; she needs one operation, done
well. Each of these narrower needs has cheaper answers than the system that
usually meets it.

\emph{Third, reverse flow.} Some of these answers turn out to be useful in
places that had the default all along, because they are cheaper, simpler or
more portable. This is the least common of the three, and the one that best
shows the solutions are more than second-best substitutes.

The figure also shows the path I most want readers to keep in mind: the arrow
from the missing default straight to going without. That arrow carries most of
the traffic.
