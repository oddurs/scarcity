% 3.3 Health
\subsection{Health}
\label{sec:health}

Health care is where the gap between the standard solution and what people can
afford is widest, and where the stakes of getting a cheaper version wrong are
highest. Three cases from India and elsewhere show the mechanism at work, and
one shows its limits.

\paragraph{The Jaipur Foot.} In the late 1960s in Jaipur, the orthopaedic
surgeon P.\,K.\ Sethi set out, with local craftsmen, to make an artificial
foot for people whose lives the imported designs did not fit. Western
prosthetic feet assumed shoes, chairs and pavements. Many of Sethi's patients
went barefoot and worked in fields. The reframing was to ask what a foot had to
\emph{do} in rural Rajasthan rather than how to copy one designed for
somewhere else. The result was built for barefoot amputees from readily
available materials, mainly vulcanised rubber; it could be made in about an
hour, and it let people work in muddy, wet fields and even climb trees
\parencite{arya2008,sethi1978}. In 1978 Sethi
and colleagues reported that it had been fitted to more than
\jaipurAmputees{} amputees, and that each foot cost only about
\jaipurCostOrig{} to make \parencite{sethi1978}. The foot is now distributed by
the charity Bhagwan Mahaveer Viklang Sahayata Samiti, free of charge to
patients. \todo{BMVSS limbs fitted count}

\paragraph{Aravind Eye Care.} Aravind began in 1976 as an
\aravindStartBeds{}-bed eye clinic based in Madurai, in southern India. It
treated cataract surgery less like a craft and more like a production process,
with standardised protocols, checklists and quality assurance, and with many
tasks shifted from surgeons to a new cadre of mid-level ophthalmic personnel,
so that surgeons' time goes mostly to operating. Fees are transparent, with
waivers for those who cannot pay \parencite{ravilla2014}. A detailed costing found that a cataract operation at Aravind cost
about \aravindCostPerOp{} in total, or \aravindCostPerQaly{} per
quality-adjusted life-year gained, and attributed the low cost to domestic
manufacturing of supplies, a specialised workforce, standardised protocols and
few regulatory hurdles \parencite{le2016aravind}. The hospital has carried out
more than \aravindSurgeries{} surgeries and laser procedures
\parencite{ravilla2014}. \todo{Aravind vs rich-country cost and quality comparison}

\paragraph{The Embrace warmer.} Premature babies need to be kept warm, and the
standard answer is an incubator: a powered, climate-controlled box that needs a
reliable electricity supply, maintenance and trained staff. In 2007 a group of
Stanford students asked what a premature baby in a rural clinic actually
needed, and the answer was warmth for a few hours at a time. They designed a
small sleeping bag with a pouch of wax that holds a steady temperature after
being heated. In 2011 it was reported to cost \embraceCostLow{} to
\embraceCostHigh{}, against up to \incubatorCost{} for a conventional
incubator \parencite{routson2011embrace}.

\begin{counterpoint}
Cost comparisons like those in \cref{tab:health-costs} flatter frugal designs
in two ways. First, much of the difference reflects wages and prices rather
than design: an operation in India costs less than one in Britain partly
because everything in India costs less. Second, the cheaper product often does
less. A rubber foot is not a carbon-fibre foot, and a wax-filled warmer cannot
do what an incubator does for a very sick baby. The evidence on outcomes is
also uneven. Aravind's results are well documented, but a survey of
paediatricians in 2014 noted that there were no published trials of the
Embrace warmer \parencite{nimbalkar2014}. \todo{NeoNurture or later Embrace evidence}
\end{counterpoint}
